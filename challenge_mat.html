The space challenge mat

Three rockets, three lanes, 190 points, for every robot the class builds.

The mat

The challenge mat is a space field for three robots at once. Each robot is a rocket. It is made of 24 sheets of letter paper taped together, 4 sheets across and 6 sheets down. It is about 86 cm wide and 168 cm tall.

The mat is for every driving robot the class builds: a robot with small wheels or big wheels, and a robot with a colour sensor, a distance sensor, a force sensor, or no sensor. Every robot can do every mission. Find your robot to see how.

The mat has three lanes: the red rocket, the blue rocket, and the green rocket. Each lane has a launch pad, a space station, an asteroid, and a moon base. The space stations are at different distances: 55 cm in lane 1, 50 cm in lane 2, and 45 cm in lane 3.

There are four missions and one bonus. Each robot that does a mission scores points. When all three robots do it on the same run, the class scores more. There are 190 points in all.

The challenge mat from above, drawn to scale. The dashed grey lines are the edges of the sheets. Each robot starts on its launch pad on the left and moves forward to the right.

Make the mat

  1. Print the file in colour at 100 % size on letter paper, with the paper upright. In the print window choose Actual size, not Fit to page.
  2. Check one sheet with a ruler. The bar in its bottom left corner is 5 cm long.
  3. Page 1 is a picture of the finished mat. The other 24 pages are the sheets. Each sheet has its name in its bottom right corner: a letter for its column and a number for its row. After the sheets come the programs for every mission, in Python and in Word Blocks, to hand to each crew.
  4. Each team tapes one lane. Lane 1 is rows 1 and 2, lane 2 is rows 3 and 4, and lane 3 is rows 5 and 6. Lay the 8 sheets out face up, edge to edge, like the picture. Do not cut anything off and do not overlap the sheets. The white strip around each sheet is part of the mat.
  5. Turn the sheets over two at a time and tape every seam on the back. Tape on the front can catch a wheel.
  6. Join the three lanes, lane 1 at the top. Put the mat on a hard, flat floor and tape its corners down.

Find your robot's numbers

Two robots with different wheels need different numbers to move the same distance. The ruler along the lane works for every robot.

  1. Put the robot on the launch pad with its front edge level with the launch line.
  2. Run a program that moves forward 360 degrees of motor turn. That is one full turn of the wheels.
  3. Read the ruler at the robot's front edge. That is how many cm one full turn moves your robot.
  4. Find your wheels in the table and start from its number. Change the number, one change for each run, until the front edge stops where the mission says.

  • The quarter turn is different for each robot too. Find it by testing, as in session 2.
  • The programs on the session pages say port.A and port.E. Those are the drive motors of the Advanced Driving Base. If your robot's drive motors are in other ports, change the two letters, left motor first. Read each letter on the hub beside the motor's cable.
  • If the robot then moves backward when the program says forward, swap the two letters.

Three crews, one score

The three teams are crews of the same space mission. They are not playing against each other. The class has one score, and the biggest points come only when all three rockets do a mission on the same run.

  • A run starts with a countdown from 5. All three crews press the button on their hub at lift off.
  • A crew whose rocket is working helps the next crew: read their numbers, watch their run, and say what you saw.
  • Crews may share numbers such as QUARTER_TURN, but each crew tests a shared number on its own robot before a scored run. Two robots built from the same book can still need different numbers, and two robots with different wheels always do.

Stop at the dock with a sensor

Every program here has the same three steps: start the wheels, wait for the sensor, stop the wheels. Only the wait changes from one sensor to the next. These are the programs for mission 3, and the first part of every mission 4 program.

A colour sensor

Start the wheels, wait until the colour sensor is over the black dock, stop the wheels. Session 3 teaches this program. DARK is a number between the sensor's reading over the white paper and its reading over the black dock. Each crew measures both on its own robot.

from hub import port
import color_sensor
import motor_pair
import runloop

LINE_SENSOR = port.B
DARK = 50


async def main():
    # The left drive motor is in port A. The right drive motor is in port E.
    motor_pair.pair(motor_pair.PAIR_1, port.A, port.E)

    # Start the wheels. Wait until the sensor is over the dock. Stop the wheels.
    motor_pair.move(motor_pair.PAIR_1, 0, velocity=250)
    await runloop.until(lambda: color_sensor.reflection(LINE_SENSOR) < DARK)
    motor_pair.stop(motor_pair.PAIR_1)


runloop.run(main())
Word Blocksthe same program as blocks

A distance sensor

The distance sensor measures how far away the thing in front of it is. The mat is flat, so the crew gives the sensor a wall to measure.

  • The wall is a box or a thick book that stands up by itself. It is at least 15 cm tall and 20 cm wide.
  • Stand the wall across the lane, facing the robot, with its face 10 cm past the dashed red line. Use a ruler. In lane 1 that is about 9 cm past the mat's right edge, in lane 2 it is about 4 cm past the mat's right edge, and in lane 3 it is about 1 cm inside the mat's right edge.
from hub import port
import distance_sensor
import motor_pair
import runloop

WALL_SENSOR = port.F
NEAR = 150


async def main():
    # The left drive motor is in port A. The right drive motor is in port E.
    motor_pair.pair(motor_pair.PAIR_1, port.A, port.E)

    # Start the wheels. Wait until the wall is near. Stop the wheels.
    motor_pair.move(motor_pair.PAIR_1, 0, velocity=250)
    await runloop.until(lambda: 0 < distance_sensor.distance(WALL_SENSOR) < NEAR)
    motor_pair.stop(motor_pair.PAIR_1)


runloop.run(main())
Word Blocksthe same program as blocks
  • NEAR = 150 is a first try. Python counts this distance in tenths of a cm, so 150 is 15 cm. A bigger number stops the robot sooner. A smaller number stops it later.
  • The sensor gives -1 when it measures nothing. That is why the test also asks for more than 0.
  • port.F is a first try. Read the letter on the hub beside the sensor's cable.

A force sensor

The force sensor has a button on its front. The wall presses the button when the robot reaches it.

  • Stand the wall with its face on the dashed red line. The wall has to be heavy, so the robot does not push it: a box with books inside works.
  • The force sensor is the part of the robot that is farthest forward, so that it reaches the wall first.

No sensor

Move forward by a number. The crew reads the lane's ruler and works the number out. Start from the number in Find your robot's numbers. After a lane swap, read the ruler of the new lane and change that one number.

A program for every mission

Each program is whole. Copy it, then change the numbers in capitals for your robot and your lane. Every number is a first try for the Advanced Driving Base in lane 1. The match

In the last session the class flies the whole space mission. A match is 3 minutes long, and all three robots are on the mat for all of it.

  • The class runs the missions in any order, one mission at a time.
  • Between missions each crew may pick its robot up, put it back on a launch pad, and choose another program on the hub.
  • Once a run starts, nobody touches a robot until all three have stopped.
  • A mission can be run again. Its best run counts.
  • One builder from each crew keeps the score for another crew's lane and reads its ruler. If a robot moves a wall, that builder puts the wall back before the next run.

Every version of the robot

  • Nothing printed on the mat is for one robot only. This printing changed the rules, the numbers, and the programs on this page. In the PDF it changed page 1 and added the program pages after the sheets. On the sheets only small things moved: the notes along each sheet's bottom edge, and the moon base label in lanes 2 and 3. No line, dock, or mark that a mission uses moved, so a mat that is already printed and taped is still right.
  • The mat was laid out for the largest class robot, about 22 cm long and 20 cm wide. A smaller robot has more room in every mission.
  • The three robots do not have to match. One run can have a robot with a colour sensor, a robot with a distance sensor, and a robot with no sensor. Only a lane whose robot needs a wall gets one.
  • A wall belongs to its robot, not to its lane. After a swap, stand it in the robot's new lane, the same distance past the dashed red line.
  • A robot with no sensor may change one number after a swap, and a robot with a sensor may change nothing. Both score the same, so no crew loses points for the robot it was given. What the sensor crews gain is time in the match.
  • The session pages are written for the Advanced Driving Base: ports A and E, and about 28 cm for one full turn. A crew with another version keeps the programs and changes the port letters and the numbers, as Find your robot's numbers says.

Where the numbers come from

One full turn of a big wheel moves the robot 27.63 cm, LEGO's own figure for the Advanced Driving Base. The small wheel of LEGO's Driving Base is 5.6 cm across, which is about 17.5 cm around. The table in Find your robot's numbers is computed from those two figures, not measured. A team finds its own number by trying, as in session 1.

Why the docks are at different distances

Session 3 moves the far line between runs to show that a set distance stops working. A printed line cannot move, so the crews move instead. After the swap in mission 3, a program that drives a set distance stops 5 or 10 cm from where it should. A program that waits for a sensor still stops at the dock, because the black dock and the wall are each the same distance from the dashed red line in every lane.

Room for three robots

  • The robots' paths are 56 cm apart. In mission 2 a robot reaches about 38 cm below its start, and in mission 4 its front edge reaches the bottom edge of its own lane. Neither reaches the next robot, which sits about 7 cm inside its own lane.
  • Every robot turns to the right, so no two robots turn toward each other.
  • If the room cannot fit a mat 168 cm long, leave the lanes apart. Each lane is a complete field 86 cm by 56 cm.

How the mat differs from the session pages

  • Session 2's mission square has sides of about 55 cm. That square does not fit a lane. On the mat the square has sides of about 28 cm, which is FORWARD = 360 on big wheels.
  • Session 4's drop zone is a square of tape 40 cm on a side. A moon base is 27 cm by 22 cm.
  • The session pages say far line, crate, and drop zone. On the mat these are the dock, the space rock, and the moon base.
  • The session pages use no distance sensor and no force sensor. The two programs for them are on this page only.

Not yet checked on a robot

  • The mat was drawn from the wheel arithmetic. The places of the moon base, the asteroid, and the home marks also assume a robot about 22 cm long and 20 cm wide whose wheels are about 11 cm behind its front edge. Nobody has run a robot of any version on this mat.
  • Print and tape one lane first. Run session 2's chunk 3 program and session 4's chunk 1 program on it. If the robot touches a crate on the asteroid, or its big wheels do not end inside the moon base, change FORWARD and tell the class the new number.
  • Read the colour sensor over the white paper, over the pale colour of a launch pad, and over the printed dock, as session 3 does for the floor and the tape. Printed black can read higher than tape. If the readings are close, lay black tape over the printed dock. No picture is printed between the launch line and the dock where the middle of the robot passes.
  • Every program on this page runs to the end against the build's stand-in for the hub, and asks for the moves its mission needs, in order. None has been run on a real hub, and no block stack was built in the SPIKE App. The distance sensor and force sensor lines follow LEGO's own Python lessons. Build and run each program once before a crew uses it, and check that 15 cm stops the robot between the dock and the dashed red line on your wall.

  • There are 23 cm of mat behind the launch line. A longer robot hangs over the mat's left edge at the start, which does no harm on a flat floor.
